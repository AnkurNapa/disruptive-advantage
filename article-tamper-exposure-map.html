Quality, safety and traceability

Mapping where someone could get at your beer

HACCP is built for accidents. Tampering is deliberate. The same step by step discipline, applied to every point where product is open to a person, shows where a brewery is exposed.

Why accident thinking misses this

A HACCP plan asks what could go wrong at each step by chance: a coolant leak, detergent left after CIP, a broken bottle at the filler. It does not ask who might do it on purpose. Tampering can come from an unhappy employee, a contractor, a visitor or an extortionist outside the gate. The motive differs. The question is the same: where can someone reach the product without being seen?

The cost is not just one batch. A credible tampering threat can force a market withdrawal on a brand regardless of whether anything was actually added. Due diligence is the legal defence here: showing you took every reasonable step. That defence needs a written exposure assessment, not a feeling that the site is secure.

Where beer is open to a person

Walk the process flow diagram and mark every point where product, or something that touches product, can be reached by hand. In a brewery the list is longer than most people expect.

  • Brewhouse additions: hop dosing, salts, finings and any manual adjunct tip.
  • Sample cocks on fermenters and bright beer tanks.
  • Dosing stations for stabilisers, colour or syrup, especially manual ones.
  • Filler bowl access during changeover and the crown or closure hopper.
  • Keg filling and returned keg handling.
  • Chemical and processing aid stores, which reach product through CIP and dosing.
  • Packaging material stores: crowns, cans and ends that will touch beer.

Scoring each point

Use the same risk levels HACCP uses, adapted for intent. For each exposure point, score how easy access is and how bad the outcome would be, on a simple 1 to 3 scale.

Exposure score per pointExposure = access (1 to 3) x consequence (1 to 3)

Access: 1 locked and logged, 2 restricted area but no log, 3 open to anyone on site. Consequence: 1 single pack, 2 one tank or run, 3 a blend reaching many packs.

Take a hypothetical brewery. The bright beer tank sample cocks sit in a cellar with badge access but no record of who opened a cock: access 2. A bright tank feeds a whole packaging run: consequence 3. Score 6. The crown hopper at the filler is in the open packaging hall, reachable by anyone who walks past: access 3. It feeds every bottle in the run: consequence 3. Score 9. The hop dosing station is locked and the key is signed out: access 1. Anything added there is boiled: consequence 2. Score 2.

Bright tank sample cocks236
Hop dosing station122

Anything at 6 or above gets a control: a lid with a seal, a locked cabinet, a camera on the point or a log of every access. Where possible, design the exposure out entirely. A closed crown feed from a locked store removes the open hopper rather than guarding it.

The traps

  • Assessing only the perimeter. Fences and gates are the easy part. Most exposure is inside, during normal work.
  • Forgetting the contractors. Shutdown weeks bring many people onto site with tools and little supervision.
  • Seals nobody checks. A tamper seal on a dosing tank is only a control if someone records that it was intact before use.
  • No way to tell trade from plant. When a foreign object turns up, you need to show whether it entered during manufacture or in trade. For a pasteurised product, an enzyme test helps. If an insect found in the pack still has active enzymes, it did not go through the pasteuriser.

What good looks like

A written exposure register covering every point on the process flow, scored and reviewed at least once a year and after any plant modification. Each high score has a named control and evidence that the control is checked. When a threat or a complaint arrives, you can show the regulator exactly what was in place on that day.
