Predictive maintenance in a brewery, priced in beer and kilowatts

Predictive maintenance pays on machines that fail expensively and log plenty of data. It disappoints on machines that failed twice in five years.

The breakdown that happens mid-run

A filler that stops in the middle of a packaging run costs more than the repair. Beer waits in the bright tank, the next brew waits for the fermenter it was meant to go into and the overtime bill arrives a week later. Predictive maintenance in a brewery means spotting a machine degrading early enough that the repair happens on a planned day instead.

The data is usually there already. Motor current, vibration, discharge temperature, oil pressure, run hours and stoppage codes sit in the PLC, the historian or the maintenance system. What is missing is anyone looking at them together before the breakdown rather than after it.

The term covers three different things. They need very different amounts of history.

ApproachWhat it doesWhat it needs
Condition limitsAlarms when a reading crosses a set limit, such as bearing temperatureLive readings and sensible limits
Anomaly detectionFlags a machine behaving unlike its own normal, before any limit is crossedWeeks to months of normal running per machine
Remaining useful lifeEstimates time to failureMany recorded failures of the same machine type

Most breweries should live in the first two rows. Very few have enough failures of one compressor model to predict remaining life honestly. Anyone who promises it should be asked how many failures they trained on.

Which machines and what the plant decides differently

  • Ammonia compressors and refrigeration. They run around the clock and carry the cellar's temperature control. A failure in summer puts every fermenter at risk. Engineering sees drift weeks ahead and books the service.
  • Fillers and seamers. Stoppages, fill level rejects and seam results by filling valve or seaming head. The pattern often points to one valve long before the line stops. Packaging fixes that valve at the next changeover.
  • Pumps. Wort, transfer, CIP and glycol pumps. Motor current against flow shows a worn impeller or a failing seal.
  • Centrifuges and filters. Differential pressure, motor load and bowl vibration. Expensive to repair and slow to replace.
  • Air and CO2 compressors and boilers. Run hours, load cycles and efficiency, which feed straight into energy cost per hectolitre.

Rank machines by cost of failure multiplied by how often it happens, not by which one has the most sensors. The filler that stops forty times a shift for a few seconds each may cost more beer and time than the compressor that never fails.

A worked example: a compressor losing efficiency

Not every problem is a breakdown. Some machines degrade slowly and cost money every hour while they do. Say the cellar's refrigeration plant delivers 300 kW of cooling. Clean and well set, it runs at a coefficient of performance (COP) of 4.0. Over a season, fouled condensers and a drifting setpoint take it to 3.4.

Compressor power for the same coolingpower (kW) = cooling (kW) / COP. At COP 4.0: 300 / 4.0 = 75.0 kW. At COP 3.4: 300 / 3.4 = 88.2 kW

Same tanks, same beer, 13.2 kW more electrical power.

If the plant carries that load for 6,000 hours a year, the extra 13.2 kW comes to about 79,400 kWh a year. Multiply by your tariff for the money. Nothing has failed. No alarm has fired. A flag on COP against its own normal would have shown the drift months earlier. The fix was a condenser clean and a setpoint check.

COP needs cooling delivered as well as compressor power, which means glycol or brine flow and temperatures in and out. Some breweries do not log all of those yet. Finding that gap early is worth more than any model, because it tells you exactly which meter to fit.

Questions to ask any provider before you buy predictive maintenance

  • How many failures does the model learn from? Two bearing failures in five years cannot train a failure model.
  • Does it know the operating mode? A pump on CIP behaves differently from the same pump on transfer. A model that does not know the mode flags every CIP and gets switched off within a week.
  • Who receives the alert at 02:00? It needs a named person and enough context to judge it: the reading, its normal range, the mode and the last work order.
  • How are false alarms recorded? That log is what turns alerts into something engineers trust.
  • What about sensor drift? A drifting vibration sensor looks exactly like a failing bearing. Calibration records should sit beside the data.
  • Does it write to the PLC? It should not.

Questions people ask

Do we need new sensors?

Often not to start. Motor current, temperatures, pressures and stoppage codes usually already sit in the PLC or historian. Some measures, such as cooling delivered for compressor efficiency, may need a flow meter. The assessment says which.

Will Beer365 change our PLC settings?

No. It reads from SCADA, the historian and the maintenance system. It never writes back to control.

Do we need a history of failures?

Not for condition limits or for anomaly flags against each machine's own normal running. Predicting time to failure needs many recorded failures, which most breweries do not have.

Which machines should we start with?

The ones that are expensive to lose and already log data, typically ammonia compressors and the filler. The assessment ranks them by cost of failure multiplied by how often it happens.
