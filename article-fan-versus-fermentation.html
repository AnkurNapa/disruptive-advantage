Is your wort feeding the yeast properly?

Free amino nitrogen is the yeast's food supply. A FAN figure on its own tells you little. Paired with the fermentation curve it explains slow ferments, diacetyl and ester swings.

Why FAN deserves a place next to the gravity curve

Yeast needs nitrogen to build new cells and most of what it can use comes as free amino nitrogen in the wort. Short it and the yeast grows less, the fermentation slows near the end and the flavour moves. Higher alpha-acetolactate means more diacetyl to clean up. The carbon to nitrogen balance shifts the ester profile. Nobody tastes FAN directly. They taste the fermentation it shaped.

The standard guidance is clear enough. Around 140 to 160 mg/L at 10 Plato is the minimum for a healthy ferment. Typical worts run 150 to 200 mg/L for ales and 180 to 220 mg/L for lagers. The trouble is that most breweries measure FAN occasionally, file the result and never put it next to the fermentation curves where it would explain something.

The question is not whether FAN is in spec. It is whether low FAN brews actually ferment differently on your plant, with your yeast.

What to collect

  • Wort FAN at knock-out or at pitching, by the ninhydrin method, with the wort gravity it was measured at
  • Beer FAN at end of primary, so you know what the yeast actually took up
  • Fermentation curve: gravity readings over time, ideally from an in-line density meter or at least twice daily
  • The confounders: pitching rate, wort oxygen, pitching temperature and yeast generation
  • Recipe facts: adjunct share and malt lot, because both move FAN

From the curve, pull a handful of features per brew: time to 50% of the apparent attenuation, time to terminal gravity, the final apparent attenuation and the VDK at the end of the rest.

FAN uptakeFAN uptake (mg/L) = wort FAN - beer FAN at end of primary
Gravity-normalised FANFAN at 10 P basis = measured FAN x 10 / wort Plato

A worked example

Say an all-malt wort at 12 Plato carries 230 mg/L FAN. The brewery moves to 30% rice adjunct, which brings extract but almost no nitrogen. As a first estimate, FAN drops in proportion to the malt share: 230 x 0.70 is about 161 mg/L. On a 10 Plato basis that is 161 x 10 / 12, about 134 mg/L, which is below the guide.

Now look at the curves. If the adjunct brews take a day longer to reach terminal gravity and carry more VDK at the end of the rest, while pitching rate and oxygen were steady, you have a nitrogen story worth acting on. If the curves are identical, your yeast copes and the guide number is conservative for your plant. Either way you now know, instead of assuming.

Measure the beer FAN as well. If uptake drops from, say, 120 mg/L to 85 mg/L on the adjunct brews, the yeast was hungry rather than lazy.

The traps

  • Proline. The ninhydrin method does not measure proline, which yeast barely uses anyway. Fine for FAN. Wrong if someone compares it with a total amino acid figure from another lab.
  • One sample a month. FAN varies with the malt lot. A monthly figure cannot explain a single slow fermentation.
  • Confounders hiding the signal. A low pitch or poor aeration slows a ferment more than modest FAN changes. Unless you hold them steady or record them, FAN gets the blame for oxygen.
  • Gravity readings with gaps. A curve with two points a day cannot show a lag that lasts six hours. Coarse data makes every ferment look the same.
  • High FAN is not free. Excess nitrogen left in the beer feeds spoilage organisms and shifts flavour. More is not automatically better.

What good looks like

Good is a simple chart that every brewer can read: FAN on one axis, time to terminal gravity or end VDK on the other, one dot per brew, coloured by adjunct share or malt lot. A healthy plant shows a floor below which the dots spread out. That floor, measured on your own yeast, becomes your FAN specification rather than a textbook number.
