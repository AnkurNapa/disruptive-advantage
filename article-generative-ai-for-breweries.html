The records nobody reads twice

A brewery writes a lot down. Batch records, SOPs, deviation reports, shift handover notes, supplier certificates, complaint emails. Almost nobody reads any of it twice. The deviation report from a stuck fermentation two summers ago is in a shared drive somewhere, written by someone who has since moved to packaging. When the same thing happens again, the team starts from zero.

That is the job generative AI does well in a brewery. It finds what was written, pulls the relevant lines together and answers a plain question such as 'what did we do the last three times FV12 stalled above 3 °P?' It saves the hour a quality manager spends opening folders. It does not replace the quality manager.

It also fails in a specific way. A language model is fluent. Asked a question it cannot answer from the records, it will still produce a confident paragraph. In a plant that pays excise and signs off release, a confident wrong paragraph is worse than no answer at all.

What a citation changes on the floor

A head brewer will not act on 'the diacetyl rest was probably short'. They will act on 'batch L2417, fermentation log, 14 March: temperature raised to 14 °C at 4.1 °P and cooled 22 hours later'. With the source on screen they can check it in ten seconds. Without it they should ignore the answer.

With citations in place, each part of the business gets something different from the same copilot.

  • Brewhouse and cellar: past batches and deviations found by symptom, tank, beer or yeast generation.
  • Quality: a draft non-conformance summary from lab results and operator notes, for a named person to check and sign.
  • Packaging: every stoppage note for a filler over a date range, in one list, with the dissolved oxygen results beside it.
  • Supply chain: a malt or hop certificate of analysis compared with the specification, with anything out of range flagged.
  • Sales and finance: volumes, freshness and margins quoted from the governed numbers, never estimated.

Numbers need the same discipline as documents. When the question is dissolved oxygen at the filler last month by line, the copilot answers from the batch record and shows which batches and tags it used. The brewer sees what sits behind the figure. That is the table version of a citation.

A worked example: has this lager had high diacetyl before?

Say a quality manager asks the copilot whether the pale lager has had high diacetyl before and what fixed it. A good answer looks for lab results above the release limit, then the fermentation log around each diacetyl rest, then any deviation report on the same batch.

Suppose it finds four batches in three years. Two had the rest cut short when the cellar was full before a holiday. One came from a slow seventh-generation yeast crop. One has no explanation in the records at all. The honest answer says exactly that, cites all four and says the fourth is unexplained. A bad copilot fills the gap with a plausible reason. That fourth batch is where trust is won or lost.

Now the follow-up: what would it cost to hold the next batch two more days? That is arithmetic over tank days, volume and the brew waiting for the vessel. It belongs in a tested formula. The copilot quotes the result of that formula and does not do the sum itself. The same goes for excise, ABV and blend figures. Release decisions and allergen wording stay with a named person working from approved records.

Retrieval and citation for what happened. Formulas for what it costs. A person for what to do about it. Most disappointment with brewery chatbots comes from asking one tool to do all three.

Questions to ask any provider before you buy a brewery copilot

  • Does every answer cite its source? Batch, document, date, tag. If not, walk away.
  • What does it say when the records are silent? The right answer is 'not in the records'.
  • Where do our records and questions go? Ask for it in writing, including whether anything is used to train a model.
  • Does it respect who can see what? A copilot that ignores permissions will not get past your plant director.
  • Who does the arithmetic? Duty, ABV and loss figures should come from defined formulas, not from the language model.
  • Can we test it on questions we already know the answers to? Before anyone relies on it.

Questions people ask

Is our data used to train a public model?

Beer365 runs the copilot inside your own Microsoft tenant, on your records, under your permissions. Ask any provider, us included, to set out in writing where prompts and records go.

Can the copilot calculate duty or ABV?

It quotes them from defined formulas over your governed numbers. It does not do the arithmetic itself, because language models can produce wrong numbers fluently.

What stops it making things up?

Every statement must cite a record. When nothing supports an answer, it says the answer is not in the records.

Do we need to clean up our documents first?

Not all of them. The assessment picks the records the first questions need. Paper brew sheets that matter may need typing into a spreadsheet first.
