Why brewery dashboards stop being opened

Power BI for breweries usually starts the same way. Someone senior asks for a production dashboard. A report appears with forty visuals on one page. It gets opened for a fortnight. Then the head brewer goes back to the whiteboard in the cellar office, because the whiteboard is right and the dashboard is a day late.

The software was rarely the problem. The problem is that nobody agreed what the numbers mean. Packaging counts production at the filler. Finance counts it at the excise register. The brewhouse counts cast wort. All three call it production, so the morning meeting spends ten minutes on whose figure is right before anyone talks about beer.

A brewery does not need to become a Power BI shop to fix this. What it needs is a set of pages, one per role, that answer the question each person asks before the morning meeting, from definitions that production and finance have both signed. That is something we deliver and run. It is not a course your team has to sit.

What each role sees and decides differently

This is the set we put in front of a mid-sized brewery. Each page carries six or seven numbers at most. Each number clicks through to the batch behind it, then to the tank, then to the readings.

RoleWhat they see each morningWhat they decide differently
Head brewerExtract and knockout gravity against target per brew, cast volume against planWhich brews to look into today, before the next mash-in repeats the fault
Cellar managerGravity against profile per tank, predicted free date, tank days per beerWhether next week's brewing plan fits the vessels that will actually be free

SalesVolume by outlet and channel, outlets whose orders are fallingWhich accounts to visit before they stop ordering
FinanceCost per packaged hectolitre, beer loss by stage, packaged volume against excise removalsWhere the margin went this month, in hectolitres rather than guesses

The plant director gets one more page with the handful of numbers that move money: cost per packaged hectolitre, loss by stage, OEE on the bottleneck line, stock cover on the top SKUs, open quality holds. If the director has to ring the cellar to find out why loss went up, the page has failed, however good it looks.

A worked example: one shift, two OEE figures

Say a can line is planned for an 8-hour shift, 480 minutes. It stops for 60 minutes in total, 30 of them for a planned CIP. Rated speed is 600 cans a minute. It makes 226,800 cans, of which 223,400 pass fill level and seam checks.

FactorCIP counted as lost timeCIP taken out of planned time
Availability420 / 480 = 87.5%420 / 450 = 93.3%
Performance226,800 / (600 x 420) = 90.0%90.0%
Quality223,400 / 226,800 = 98.5%98.5%
OEE77.6%82.7%

Both figures are arithmetically correct. If packaging quotes one and finance quotes the other, the meeting loses fifteen minutes and nobody fixes the filler. The cure is not a better chart. It is one written decision, made by the people who own the line and the budget, on whether planned cleaning counts as lost time. Once it is signed, every page in the building uses it and the argument does not come back.

Most of the real work in a brewery dashboard project is decisions like that one. Rated speed per SKU, because a line validated at 600 cans a minute on 330 ml may run slower on 500 ml. Which loss belongs to which stage. Whether a blend counts as one batch or two. We bring the list of decisions. Your people make them.

Questions to ask any provider before you buy brewery dashboards

  • Whose tenant does it run in? If the reports live in the provider's account, you are renting your own numbers.
  • Who signs the definitions? Production and finance should sign them, not the developer.
  • Can every number click through to a batch? If it cannot, nobody will trust it the first time it looks odd.
  • How current is each page? Every page should show when each source last loaded.
  • What will licences and capacity cost each year? Get that in writing, separate from the project fee.
  • Who changes a page when your process changes? Ask how long it takes and what it costs.
  • What happens when you leave? You should keep every report, model and record without a migration.
