Why most brewery dashboards stop being opened

Power BI for breweries usually starts the same way. Someone senior asks for a production dashboard. An analyst builds forty visuals on one page. It gets opened for a fortnight, then the head brewer goes back to the whiteboard in the cellar office because the whiteboard is right and the dashboard is a day late.

The dashboards that survive share three habits. Each one is built for a role, not a department. Each answers the question that role asks before the morning meeting. Every number on it comes from the same governed definitions as every other dashboard in the building, so the packaging lead and the finance director quote the same packaged volume. The third habit matters most. Two dashboards with two definitions of production do more damage than none.

Which Power BI dashboards does a brewery need?

This is the set I would build for a mid-sized brewery, one page per role, each with no more than six or seven numbers above the fold.

RoleThe morning questionMeasures on the page
Head brewerDid yesterday's brews hit gravity and volume? Which ones did not?Brewhouse yield per brew, cast volume against plan, knockout gravity against target, brews per day
Cellar managerWhich tanks are on time, which are late and what is free next week?Gravity curve against profile per tank, predicted terminal date, tank days per beer, free vessel calendar
Packaging leadWhat stopped the lines and why?OEE per line, stoppage minutes by cause, packaged volume against plan, fill level and seam results
Quality headIs anything drifting before it fails?Lab results against spec with control limits, open deviations, sensory panel scores, micro by sample point
Supply chainDo we have enough of each SKU and its materials?Stock cover in weeks by SKU, forecast against actual, malt, hop and packaging material cover
SalesWhich accounts and SKUs are moving?Volume and revenue per hectolitre by channel, depletions by account, SKU mix
Finance and plant directorWhat did a hectolitre cost and where did beer go?Cost per packaged hectolitre, beer loss by stage, energy and water per hectolitre, duty paid against packaged

Notice that the cellar page carries a predicted terminal date. That is where machine learning earns a place on a dashboard: as one honest column next to the tank, not as a separate AI page nobody visits.

The plant director's page deserves its own note, because it is where dashboards usually go wrong. The temptation is to put every other page's headline on it. Resist that. A plant director needs the handful of numbers that move money: cost per packaged hectolitre, beer loss by stage, OEE on the bottleneck line, stock cover on the top SKUs, open quality holds. Each one should click through to the role page that explains it. If the director has to ring the cellar to find out why loss went up, the page has failed, however good it looks.

What should not be on any of these pages? Vanity totals with no target. Gauges that are always green. Anything nobody in the room can act on this week. A cellar manager cannot change last year's revenue. Give them tank days.

A worked example: OEE on the filler

OEE is the packaging lead's main number. It is also the one most often calculated three different ways in the same brewery. Say a can line is planned for an 8-hour shift, 480 minutes. It stops for 60 minutes in total. Its rated speed is 600 cans a minute. It produces 226,800 cans, of which 223,400 pass fill level and seam checks.

Overall equipment effectivenessOEE = availability x performance x quality

Availability = run time / planned time. Performance = actual output / (rated speed x run time). Quality = good units / total units.

FactorCalculationResult
Availability420 min / 480 min87.5%
Performance226,800 / (600 x 420) = 226,800 / 252,00090.0%
Quality223,400 / 226,80098.5%
OEE0.875 x 0.900 x 0.98577.6%

A quick check: 223,400 good cans against the 288,000 the line could have made in 480 minutes at rated speed is also 77.6 percent. If your OEE measure does not pass that check, the DAX is wrong.

The traps are in the inputs, not the formula. Is a planned CIP inside planned time or outside it? Does a changeover count as a stop? Is rated speed the nameplate or the speed the line was validated at for this SKU? Write the answers down once, put them in the semantic model and every page inherits them.

One more check on any OEE page: make sure the line's rated speed is held per SKU. A can line that runs 600 cans a minute on 330 ml may be validated at a lower speed on 500 ml. Using one rated speed for both makes the larger pack look like a performance problem when it is a definition problem.

How to build Power BI for a brewery so the numbers agree

  • One semantic model, many reports. Measures such as packaged volume, beer loss and OEE are defined once in a shared model. Each role's report reads from it. Nobody writes their own version in a report.
  • Brewed, packaged and sold are three measures. Name them that way. Never let a visual say production.
  • Row-level security by site or area. Each brewer sees their own tanks. Cost data stays with the roles that need it.
  • Direct Lake on Fabric where the volume is large. It reads Delta tables in OneLake without a scheduled import, so the cellar page is not a day behind.
  • Raw historian data stays out of the model. Billions of readings belong in a time-series store. The model gets the per-batch summary.

Two smaller habits help. Put the definition of each measure in its description so it shows when someone hovers over it. Show the time of the last data load on every page, because a brewer who sees 06:10 trusts the page more than one who has to guess.

Plan for questions the pages do not answer. Someone will always want brewhouse yield for one beer over one quarter, split by malt lot. With a well-built semantic model, that is a filter or a plain-English question to a data agent reading the same model, not a new report request that waits three weeks.

What good looks like

A good brewery dashboard set is boring in the best way. The morning meeting opens the same pages every day. Nobody brings a spreadsheet to argue with the dashboard. When the packaging lead says OEE was 77.6 percent, finance does not reply with 81. When a number looks odd, someone can click from the loss figure to the batch that caused it, then to the tank, then to the readings.

Questions people ask

What KPIs should a brewery track in Power BI?

It depends on the role. Head brewers track brewhouse yield and knockout gravity, cellar managers track tank days and fermentation against profile, packaging tracks OEE and stoppages, quality tracks results against spec while finance tracks cost per packaged hectolitre and beer loss by stage.

How do you calculate OEE for a brewery filler?

OEE is availability x performance x quality. Availability is run time over planned time, performance is actual output over rated speed times run time, quality is good units over total units. Agree first whether CIP and changeovers count as planned time.

Why do brewery dashboards show different production numbers?

Usually because brewed, packaged and sold volume are all being called production, or each report defines the measure itself. A single shared semantic model with named, written definitions fixes it.

Should a brewery use Power BI Direct Lake?

If the data sits in Microsoft Fabric and the volumes are large, Direct Lake reads Delta tables in OneLake without a scheduled import, which keeps cellar and packaging pages current. Raw historian streams should still be summarised before they reach the model.
