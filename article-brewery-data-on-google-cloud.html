Why breweries on Google Cloud get stuck

Breweries usually arrive on Google Cloud through the commercial side. E-commerce, distributor portals, marketing and sales reporting land in BigQuery because that is where the analytics team was comfortable. The production side lives somewhere else: brew sheets in Excel, the historian on site, the ERP in its own database.

So the forecast can be right and the beer can still be missing from the warehouse. The sales data knows what will sell. It does not know that the wheat beer needed for festival week is still in a fermenter, or that the bright tank it needs is booked for something else. That gap is not a cloud problem. It is a join nobody has made.

The usual answer from a provider is to move everything into one cloud first. That puts a year of migration between the brewery and its first useful number. The commercial team loses tools that work. The brewing side waits. We think the order should be the other way round: answer the question first, on the data where it sits.

A worked example: festival week

Say a brewery sells a wheat beer that averages 800 hL a week. Three years of sales history show demand rising for a regional festival. The forecast for festival week is 1,200 hL. Stock on hand is 3,000 hL.

Weeks of stock covercover (weeks) = stock on hand / forecast weekly demand = 3,000 hL / 1,200 hL = 2.5 weeks

At the normal 800 hL a week the same stock reads as 3,000 / 800 = 3.75 weeks.

The gap between 3.75 and 2.5 weeks is the gap between a relaxed planner and a brewhouse that should have brewed a fortnight ago. A wheat beer brewed today will not be packaged for a couple of weeks at best. The forecast only helps if it sits next to tank availability and the brewing plan, in front of the person who decides what to brew next.

The forecast is also only as honest as its history. If a stock-out week is recorded as low demand, the forecast learns that festivals are quieter than they are. How to treat those weeks is a sales and supply chain decision. Your people sign it off, not ours.

That is what the brewery decides differently once the two sides meet. The planner sees cover by SKU against the date each tank will be free. Sales sees which outlets will run short. Finance sees what a missed festival week costs in hectolitres not sold.

How Beer365 reads Google Cloud data

Beer365 runs on Microsoft Azure and Microsoft Fabric inside your own Microsoft tenant. It reaches Google Cloud in two ways, chosen per source.

Where the data sitsHow Beer365 reads itWhat your IT team should know
Files in Google Cloud StorageA read-only OneLake shortcut that points at the bucketNothing is copied by default. Access uses an HMAC key for an account with read and list rights on the bucket. Fabric can cache files for 1 to 28 days to cut repeated egress.
Tables in BigQueryBigQuery mirroring into OneLakeA copy kept in step with BigQuery, typically about ten minutes behind. Microsoft does not charge Fabric compute for the replication. Storage is free up to a limit set by capacity. BigQuery's own change tracking and storage costs still apply on the Google side.

The choice between the two is practical. Tables that already live in BigQuery, such as depletions by outlet, suit mirroring. Files in buckets, such as distributor sales drops or historian exports, suit a shortcut. Either way the brewing side, read from Excel, the ERP, SCADA, the historian and the LIMS, joins the same batch record inside your tenant. Nothing is written back to any of them.

One point to plan for. Security rules set inside BigQuery do not travel with a mirror. They are set again in Fabric, matching the rules you already have, as part of the proof of concept. The work is small. Forgetting it is not.

Questions to ask any provider when you run on Google Cloud

  • Does it need a migration? If it does, ask what that gives the brewery that reading in place does not.
  • Is each source copied or read in place? Both are fine. You should know which, source by source.
  • How current will it be? A mirror about ten minutes behind is fine for planning. It is not an alarm.
  • Who rebuilds the security rules? Mirrored tables do not inherit BigQuery permissions.
  • What will it cost on both sides? Google charges for egress and change tracking. Microsoft charges for capacity.
  • Who owns the batch record at the end? It should already sit in your tenant.

Questions people ask

Do we have to leave Google Cloud?

No. Beer365 reads Cloud Storage in place through a read-only shortcut and mirrors the BigQuery tables it needs. Your Google Cloud stack stays as it is.

Is our BigQuery data copied?

Mirrored tables are. The copy sits in OneLake inside your own tenant, kept in step with BigQuery and typically about ten minutes behind. Files in Cloud Storage are read in place.

Will our BigQuery permissions carry over?

No. Mirroring does not bring BigQuery's granular security with it. The same rules are set again in Fabric during the proof of concept.

Who pays the Google Cloud costs?

You do, on your Google account, for egress and BigQuery change tracking. The assessment estimates them alongside the Fabric capacity cost.
