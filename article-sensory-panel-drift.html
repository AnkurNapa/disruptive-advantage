Quality, safety and traceability

Is your sensory panel still calibrated, or just still turning up?

A trained panel is a measuring instrument made of people. Like any instrument it drifts. The tasting sheets already hold the data to show who is still finding the faults.

The instrument nobody calibrates

Every brewery calibrates its pH meter. Very few calibrate the panel that signs off every batch for release. Yet the panel is the final quality gate for flavour. Flavour is what consumers complain about. No chemical model fully describes beer flavour. Around twenty compounds are worth routine analysis and the rest is the human nose.

Panels drift quietly. Tasters get used to the house beer and stop noticing a creeping fault. A good taster moves shift and stops attending. New members join after one training session. The panel still meets every morning. Whether it can still tell a diacetyl spike from a clean beer is a separate question.

What to measure

You already have most of the data if your panel tastes blind with spiked samples now and then. Three measures per taster cover most of it.

  • Detection: in triangle tests against a spiked sample, how often does the taster pick the odd one?
  • Identification: when they detect it, do they name the right compound?
  • Repeatability: when the same beer is served twice blind in one session, do they score it the same?

Spike at a level a trained palate should catch, using pure flavour standards in the house beer. Diacetyl just above its threshold of about 0.1 mg/L is a good test. So is a light strike sample.

The arithmetic of a triangle test

In a triangle test the taster gets three glasses, two the same and one different. A taster guessing at random picks the odd one a third of the time. So the question for each taster is whether their record beats chance by enough to mean something.

Probability of k or more correct by guessingP(X >= k) = sum over i from k to n of C(n, i) x (1/3)^i x (2/3)^(n - i)

If P is below 0.05, the taster is detecting the difference, not guessing.

Say a hypothetical taster does 12 triangle tests with diacetyl spikes over a quarter. Getting 8 or more right has a guessing probability of 0.019, below 0.05, so 8 of 12 shows real detection. Getting 7 right has a guessing probability of 0.066, which is not significant. That one glass is the difference between a taster you rely on and one you retrain.

For descriptive scores on a 0 to 10 scale, compare each taster to the panel on the same sample.

Taster bias against the panelZ = (taster score - panel mean) / panel standard deviation

Inside plus or minus 2 is normal spread. Outside it, on the same attribute, session after session, is drift.

If the panel scores bitterness on the reference beer at a mean of 5.0 with a standard deviation of 0.8, a taster who scores 6.8 sits at Z = (6.8 - 5.0) / 0.8 = 2.25. Once is noise. Five sessions in a row is a calibration problem. The fix is usually a short session with the reference beer and a pure bitterness standard, not a lecture.

Traps

  • Too few tests per taster. Four triangle tests cannot separate skill from luck. Plan enough to reach a meaningful count each quarter.
  • Spiking only easy faults. If every spike is butter, you learn nothing about cardboard or sulphur.
  • Discussing before scoring. Open discussion has its place after individual scores are written, never before.
  • Panel mean drift. If the whole panel slides together, Z scores stay small while the panel goes blind as one. Track the panel's mean on the reference beer over time as well.

What good looks like

Each taster has a record of detection, identification and repeatability per fault family, updated after every session. Release decisions lean on tasters whose record shows they detect the relevant faults. Retraining targets the specific compounds each person misses. The panel's own reference scores are trended month by month.
