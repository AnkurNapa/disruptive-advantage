The brewery already writes it all down

A working brewery produces more data than most data projects ever use. Every brew leaves a brew sheet. Every fermenter has a gravity log. The lab records every analysis. The PLCs log every temperature and valve. The ERP records every malt delivery, cost and stock movement. The excise register records every hectolitre that leaves under bond. Distributors send volumes by outlet. Maintenance logs every work order.

The problem is not quantity. Each record was written for its own job and never designed to meet the others. The brew sheet uses the brew number. The cellar uses the tank and a fermentation number. The filler uses a lot code. The ERP uses a material batch. The excise register uses a removal document. The beer is the same. The keys are not.

A brewer also knows things no system records: that tank 9 was renumbered after an expansion, or that the night shift writes gravity as specific gravity while days write °Plato. Those details decide whether a join is right. That is why your brewers sign off the mapping, not us.

The mess is where the brewing actually happened. Before anyone sells you a new system, it is worth knowing what the old ones could already tell you if they were joined.

None of those answers comes from one source. Extract needs the brew sheet and the malt certificate. Cost per hectolitre needs the ERP, loss by stage and packaged volume. Each answer is a join.

Then there are the sources nobody lists. Energy and water meters read by hand once a week. Sensory panel sheets that show a beer drifting long before a customer notices. Complaint emails that can be traced to a packaging lot if someone asked for the lot code. Shift handover notes recording the pump that sounded wrong. None of it is glamorous. All of it is already paid for.

A worked example: excise volume against packaged volume

The excise register is the most underused record in many breweries. It is kept carefully because the law requires it, then filed. Yet it is an independent count of beer leaving the brewery, which makes it an excellent check on everything upstream.

Say a brewery packaged 10,000 hL in a month according to the filler counts. The excise register shows 9,850 hL removed on duty. Bonded stock of packaged beer rose by 120 hL over the month.

Unexplained differencedifference = packaged - removed on duty - increase in bonded stock = 10,000 - 9,850 - 120 = 30 hL

30 / 10,000 = 0.3 percent of packaged volume.

Thirty hectolitres is small. It may have an innocent answer: breakage, samples, a pallet counted twice. It still needs an answer, because the excise authority may ask for one. Tracked monthly by SKU and pack, the same sum shows which line or pack format carries the gap. Joined to brewhouse and cellar records, it gives the brewery a full loss chain from cast wort to duty paid. Finance stops estimating. The packaging lead knows which line to look at.

Questions to ask any provider about your existing data

  • Do we need new systems before we start? For the first questions you should not.
  • Will anything be written back to our ERP or SCADA? It should not.
  • Do we have to digitise ten years of paper? Six months of the records the first question needs is usually enough.
  • Who maps the keys between systems and can we see the mapping? It is the most valuable table in the brewery. It should be yours.
  • Is the original record left untouched? It should always be there to check against.
