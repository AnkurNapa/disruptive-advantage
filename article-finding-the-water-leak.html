Which CIP set or hose is losing the water? Map it meter by meter

Water to beer ratio tells you the site is thirsty. It does not tell you where. Area meters, CIP records and a night flow test usually do.

Why the site total is not enough

Every brewery tracks water in hectolitres per hectolitre of beer. It is a good headline and a poor diagnostic. When it drifts up, the site total cannot tell you whether the cause is a final rinse running twice as long as the recipe, a pasteuriser make-up valve stuck open or a hose left running on the cellar floor since the morning shift.

Water costs you twice: once to buy and treat it, again to send it to effluent. The second cost is easy to forget and often the larger one.

Build the balance first

Start with the site incomer and every area meter you have: brewhouse, cellars, CIP sets, packaging, utilities, the canteen. Read them all at the same time, daily at minimum and hourly if the meters log. Then do the arithmetic nobody likes doing.

Unaccounted waterUnaccounted (m³) = site incomer - sum of area meters

Express it as a percentage of the incomer and trend it daily.

CIP rinse varianceVariance (m³) = metered rinse volume - recipe rinse volume, per cycle, per circuit

The second equation needs the CIP system's own records: which circuit ran, which vessel, which step, how long. Most CIP controllers log this already. Join it to the CIP supply meter by timestamp and each cycle gets a water cost.

A worked example

Say the incomer reads 1,200 m³ for the day and the area meters add up to 1,050 m³. That is 150 m³ unaccounted, 12.5 percent. Too big to be meter error alone, so something unmetered is drawing.

Next, a night flow test. Between 01:00 and 05:00 on a non-production night nothing should draw except known users. If the incomer shows 6 m³/h through those hours, that is 24 m³ over the four hours with nothing running. A 25 mm hose left open at 30 litres a minute is 1.8 m³/h. Three of them would explain most of it.

Then the CIP records. Suppose the fermenter final rinse recipe calls for 2.0 m³ and the meter shows 3.1 m³ on one circuit, four times a day. That is 4.4 m³ a day from one circuit, usually from a rinse that runs to drain until conductivity clears instead of to a fixed volume, or a return pump that cannot keep up so the step is extended.

The IBD hygiene notes ask for every CIP hose to be numbered and registered. Most breweries do it for hygiene and stop there. Do it for water too: record which hose is connected to which station. A running hose becomes a named hose. A hose that turns up in the leak list every week is a hose that needs a trigger gun or a new home.

The traps

  • Meters nobody checked. An area meter reading 8 percent low creates a phantom leak. Prove the meters against each other with a known volume, such as a tank fill, before you go hunting.
  • Totaliser resets and units. Some meters read litres, some m³, some roll over at a fixed count. A reset on one meter looks like a flood.
  • Recovery tanks overflowing. A CIP rinse recovery tank sent to drain on high level looks like normal use in the CIP log and like loss in the balance.
  • Comparing days that are not alike. A day with three brews and a filler changeover is not a fair comparison with a day of maintenance. Normalise by production or compare like days.

What good looks like

Good is a daily balance with unaccounted water under a few percent, a night flow number that is close to zero, a CIP cycle list with water per cycle against the recipe plus a hose register with locations. The question then answers itself in a sentence: circuit 3 final rinse, or hose 14 in cellar two.
