What a brewery data platform is for

The questions that cost a brewery money cross systems. Beer loss needs cast volume from the brewhouse, transfers from the cellar, counts from the filler and removals from the excise register. Fermentation time needs historian readings, yeast records and lab results. Cost per packaged hectolitre needs all of that plus the ERP.

In most breweries one person rebuilds that join in Excel every month. We have seen a spreadsheet called LOSSES_FINAL_v3 become the most trusted number in the building, because nobody else could reproduce it. When its owner goes on leave, the number goes with them. That person is not the problem. They are doing a job the systems never did.

A data platform exists to make that join once, under written rules, so everyone reads the same answer. That is the whole job. A platform that lands twenty sources before anyone uses one number is a storage bill with a project plan attached.

What changes once the batch record exists

On Microsoft Azure and Microsoft Fabric, a brewery's data can sit in one place inside its own tenant, with Power BI, the analytics and the copilot all reading the same copy. The plant does not notice the platform. It notices what people stop arguing about.

  • Loss by stage. The cellar sees that tank bottoms on one beer account for most of its loss, with the batches listed.
  • Tank days. Minutes out of fermentation profile per tank and a predicted free date, instead of raw historian readings nobody opens.
  • Cost per packaged hectolitre. Finance sees extract, energy, water and beer loss in the cost, not materials alone.
  • Quality against process. A dissolved oxygen result at the filler sits next to the transfer and the tank it came from.
  • Excise. Packaged volume reconciled against removals on duty every month, by SKU and pack.

The historian is a good example of why this matters. A brewery logging a few hundred tags once a second produces billions of readings a year. Nobody in the cellar wants them. They want the summary per batch: how long each stage took, the peak temperature, the minutes out of profile. The raw readings stay where they are for whoever needs to look closer. Beer365 puts the summary on the batch record, where the cellar manager and the quality head both read it.

Alerts land with a named person. If a fermenter runs 1 °C above its profile for 30 minutes, the cellar manager hears about it during the shift rather than in Friday's report. The decision is still theirs. They just get to make it while it still matters.

What good looks like

A good brewery data platform looks dull from the outside. The cellar report and the finance pack agree. A new question, such as loss by malt supplier, takes days rather than a project. The historian, the ERP and the LIMS are still the systems of record. Nobody had to replace them.

Security follows your own rules. The data stays in your Microsoft tenant under your own Microsoft Entra identities. A site brewer sees their own site's tanks. Cost data stays with the roles that need it. Those rules hold whether someone opens a report or asks the copilot a question.

When a platform goes wrong it is nearly always the same story. The key that links brew number, fermentation number and packaging lot was skipped to get a dashboard out early. Blends and split packaging runs were left for later. Everything built on top inherits the gap, so every number is right until someone checks it against the cellar log.

Questions to ask any provider before you buy a data platform

  • Does it run in our tenant or yours? That decides who owns the data on the day the contract ends.
  • Do we have to replace the ERP, LIMS or historian? For analytics you should not.
  • Does anything write back to SCADA or the PLCs? It should not.
  • Who maps the batch keys and can we see the mapping? Ask specifically about blends and split packaging runs.
  • Which question gets answered first and by when? A platform with no first question has no finish line.
  • What will Fabric capacity cost each year? In writing, separate from the fees.
  • Who signs off that the number is right? Someone in your finance team should, against a baseline agreed before work starts.
