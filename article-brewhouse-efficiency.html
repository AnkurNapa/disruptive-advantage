Brewhouse efficiency is three different numbers, and your team is quoting all of them

Mash efficiency, lauter efficiency and brewhouse yield get used interchangeably in most breweries. That is why the figure in the report never matches the figure in the head brewer's notebook.

Ankur Napa12 min readMarch 2026

On this page

  1. Three numbers, one word
  2. Where each one is measured
  3. Why the report disagrees
  4. Pinning it down

Three numbers, one word

Ask three people in a brewery what the efficiency was on a brew and you will get three answers, all of them correct, none of them the same. The word covers at least three distinct measurements, and they are taken at different points with different denominators.

MeasureWhat it comparesWhere it is taken
Mash conversionExtract in the mash against the theoretical maximum from the gristEnd of the mash rest
Lauter efficiencyExtract recovered into the kettle against extract present in the mashEnd of runoff
Brewhouse yieldExtract in cold wort at knockout against the total extract chargedInto the fermenter

Only the third one is a plant figure. The first two are process diagnostics. Reporting any of them as the efficiency without saying which is how a brewery ends up arguing about a number rather than about a process.

Where each one is measured

Extract is mass, not volume, so every one of these calculations has to run in base units before it is turned into a percentage. Take the volume at a known temperature, correct the gravity to that temperature, convert specific gravity to degrees Plato, and multiply through by density to get kilograms of extract. Skipping the temperature correction is the single commonest source of a yield figure that drifts by a point or two for no visible reason.

Extract mass in a vessel kg extract = volume (L) × SG × °P / 100

Where the gravity has already been corrected to the reference temperature the hydrometer or meter was calibrated at.

The theoretical maximum comes from the grist: each malt carries a fine grind dry basis extract figure from its certificate of analysis, adjusted for moisture. Multiply that by the mass charged and you have the extract that was theoretically available. Brewhouse yield is simply what arrived in the fermenter divided by that.

Why the report disagrees

Three things account for almost every discrepancy we see. The first is the temperature correction above. The second is the volume itself: a tank calibration chart that has not been checked since installation, or a level probe reading a foamy surface, will move the number more than any process change. The third is timing. If the volume is read at the end of knockout but the gravity is sampled from the kettle, the two figures describe different liquids.

None of these are process problems. They are measurement problems, and they are the reason a brewery can chase a yield improvement for a quarter and find that the gain was in the spreadsheet rather than the vessel.

Pinning it down

Name the measure every time it is quoted. Fix the point of measurement for each one and record it with the reading rather than assuming it. Correct gravity to temperature at the point of capture, not later. Recalibrate volume against a known fill at least annually. And compute in mass throughout, converting to a percentage only at the last step.

Do that and the three numbers stop competing. Mash conversion tells you about the grist and the rest. Lauter efficiency tells you about the bed and the sparge. Brewhouse yield tells you what the plant produced. All three are useful. Only one of them belongs on the report to the board.
