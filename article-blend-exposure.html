Fermentation and cellar

If one brew is bad, how much beer is affected

A single faulty brew rarely stays single. Fermenters merge brews, bright tanks merge fermenters and heels carry into the next fill. Exposure is a forward trace and it has two answers.

Why the answer is never one brew

The phone call goes like this. The lab has found something in brew 2 of last Tuesday, a forced VDK out of spec or a micro result nobody likes. The plant director asks the obvious question: how much beer is that? The honest answer, in most breweries, is that someone will get back to you tomorrow with a spreadsheet.

The delay matters. A hold that is too small lets bad beer into trade. A hold that is too big locks up good stock, misses dispatches and makes the next hold harder to argue for. Exposure is a quality question with a warehouse attached.

Trace forward, then weigh

Exposure is the forward version of a date code trace. Start at the suspect brew and follow every place its beer went:

  • Brew to fermenter. Large fermenters often hold several brews, so one brew is a share of a tank.
  • Fermenter to bright beer tank. Blends merge fermenters, so one tank is a share of a blend.
  • Bright beer tank to packaging runs. One tank can feed cans, bottles and kegs on different lines and days.
  • Heel to the next fill. Whatever stays in the bright tank goes into the next blend unless someone dumps it.

That trace gives the first answer, the volume the brew touched. The second answer needs the blend arithmetic: how much of the fault is still there after dilution.

Blend value of a measured attributeBlend value = sum(V_i x value_i) / sum(V_i)

Volume-weighted. Valid for concentrations such as VDK, colour or bitterness, not for pH or gravity.

A worked trace

Say FV-B holds four brews of 175 hL, 700 hL in total. Its forced total VDK comes back at 0.32 mg/L against a 0.10 mg/L spec. FV-B went into a bright beer tank with FV-A (600 hL at 0.06 mg/L) and FV-C (500 hL at 0.05 mg/L).

Blend VDK = (600 x 0.06 + 700 x 0.32 + 500 x 0.05) / 1,800 = 285 / 1,800 = 0.158 mg/L. The blend is out of spec and above the flavour threshold for diacetyl, which sits around 0.08 to 0.10 mg/L. So the whole tank is exposed, not 700 hL of it.

Touched volume: 1,775 hL packaged plus the next blend that received the heel. Now weigh that heel. If the next tank is 1,600 hL of good beer at 0.05 mg/L, its VDK becomes (25 x 0.158 + 1,600 x 0.05) / 1,625 = 0.052 mg/L. Traced, yes. Affected, no. Report it as touched and cleared, show the arithmetic and do not hold it.

Where exposure figures go wrong

Blend records on paper. If the fermenter-to-tank transfer is written on a sheet and keyed later, the forward trace stops at the cellar door. This is the single most common break.

Mid-run tank switches. A line that changes bright tank halfway through a run has two sources under one run number. Unless the switch time is recorded, both tanks are exposed for the whole run.

Recovered beer. Beer recovered from yeast or filter push-outs and dosed back into later blends carries the fault with it. Treat recovery tanks as vessels in the trace.

Averaging the wrong thing. Volume weighting works for concentrations. It does not work for pH, which blends on hydrogen ion. Nor for original gravity, which blends on extract mass. Use the right rule for each attribute or the second answer is wrong.

Stopping at packaging. Exposure that ends at run numbers tells quality what to hold. Logistics needs to know how much of it is still in the warehouse and how much has shipped, by customer.

What good looks like

Good is two numbers in minutes rather than one number in a day: the volume the brew touched and the volume where the fault is still above threshold, each with the trace and the arithmetic behind it, split by format and by stock location. Holds become proportionate. The argument about them is about the beer, not the spreadsheet.
