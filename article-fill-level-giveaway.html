How many free millilitres is your filler giving away

Fillers are set to overfill because underfilling is illegal. How much they overfill depends on how much the heads vary. Shrink the spread and the free beer shrinks with it.

Free beer, by design

No packaging manager wants an underfilled bottle on a shelf. Underfills are a legal metrology problem, a retailer problem and a reputation problem. So fillers are set to fill a little high. Nobody questions it, because a few millilitres feels like nothing.

It is not nothing. A few millilitres multiplied by every container on every line, every day, adds up to whole tanks of finished beer handed out for free. That beer has carried every cost the brewery has: malt, hops, energy, tank time, filtration. The only cost it skipped is the one you would have charged for it.

What to measure

Give-away is the gap between what you put in and what you declared, averaged over everything you packed.

Give-away for a periodGive-away (L) = (mean fill (mL) - declared volume (mL)) x containers filled / 1,000
Where the mean has to sitTarget mean = declared volume + k x standard deviation of fill

k is set so the share of underfills stays inside what your rules and your own policy allow.

The second formula is the one that matters. The mean is not a free choice. It is pushed up by the spread between heads. Cut the spread and the mean can come down without a single extra underfill.

There are two ways to get the data:

  • Fill height inspection on the line, which sees every container but measures height, not volume.
  • Check weighing: weigh full containers, subtract the tare and convert by beer density. Fewer containers, but a true volume.

For check weighing, density matters. A finished beer at a specific gravity of about 1.010 has a density near 1.008 g/mL at 20 C, so a 336 mL fill weighs about 338.8 g of beer, not 336 g.

A worked line

Say a line fills 2,000,000 bottles of 330 mL a month. The check weigher shows a mean of 336 mL with a standard deviation of 3.0 mL across heads. The plant runs a target of declared plus two standard deviations: 330 + 2 x 3.0 = 336 mL.

Give-away is (336 - 330) x 2,000,000 / 1,000 = 12,000 L. That is 120 hL a month.

Now fix the worst heads, say worn fill valves or vent tubes cut to slightly different lengths. The standard deviation falls to 2.0 mL. The same rule puts the target at 330 + 2 x 2.0 = 334 mL. Give-away becomes 4 x 2,000,000 / 1,000 = 8,000 L, which is 80 hL. The 40 hL a month difference comes from maintenance, not from accepting any extra risk of underfill.

Kegs follow the same arithmetic at larger scale. Container capacity itself varies: an aluminium keg sold as 50 L can be specified at 50.4 L plus or minus 0.3 L. It changes when dented. Fill kegs by meter or weight, never by the keg's nominal volume.

Traps

Height is not volume. Glass bottles vary in capacity. Two bottles at the same fill height can hold different volumes. Use height inspection to catch outliers and check weighing to measure give-away.

One tare for all bottles. Glass weight varies more than people expect. Weigh empty containers in the same sample. Otherwise the give-away figure carries the glass variation inside it.

Foam and temperature. Weighing a fobbing container or reading levels on warm beer gives a different answer from a settled one. Standardise when and how samples are taken.

Mean only. A line can hit its mean while one head underfills and another overfills. Report by head, as well as by line.

What good looks like

Good is give-away reported in hectolitres a month, by line and by head, next to the standard deviation that justifies the target. The conversation moves from whether a few millilitres matter to which heads are forcing the target up. That is a maintenance list, with a volume of beer attached to each item.
