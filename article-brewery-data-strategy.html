A brewery data strategy that starts with one question worth money

A brewery data strategy does not start with a platform or an AI pilot. It starts with one question that costs money every week and someone who will sign the answer.

Why brewery data strategies stall

Most brewery data strategies stall in one of two places. Some start with the platform: a year of migration, twenty sources landed, no number anyone uses. Others start with the AI pilot: a clever model on one tank's data that impresses in a meeting and never runs again because nobody can feed it. Both skip the dull middle. That middle is the batch record, the definitions and one question the brewery actually argues about.

Neither failure is anyone's fault. Both are what happens when the plan is written around a tool rather than around the beer.

The strategy that works is staged. Each stage ends with a number used in a real meeting. Nothing moves on until the last stage is trusted. It is slower in the first month and much faster by the sixth, because nobody has to go back and rebuild the foundations.

Where a brewery should start

Start with a question that passes three tests. It costs money every week. Someone senior already asks it. The data to answer it exists, even if it is scattered across Excel, the ERP and a lab book. Good first questions in a brewery:

  • How much beer do we lose and where? Brewhouse, cellar, filtration, packaging. Every brewery has a loss number. Few can split it by stage.
  • Why do some fermentations run long? Tank days are the constraint in many breweries running near capacity.
  • What stops the filler? Stoppage codes exist in the PLC. They are rarely summarised by cause and SKU.
  • What does a packaged hectolitre really cost? Including loss, energy and water, not materials alone.

Pick one. Not four. Beer loss is often a good first choice because answering it needs brewhouse, cellar, packaging and excise data joined by batch, which is exactly the foundation every later question needs. If two candidates look equally good, choose the one someone can act on within a month. A loss figure that points to tank bottoms on one beer changes the cellar routine next week. A five-year capacity model changes nothing until next year's budget.

A worked example: sizing the first question

Say a brewery casts 100,000 hL of wort a year and reports total process loss, from cast wort to packaged beer, of 8 percent. Nobody can say where it goes.

Annual loss in hectolitresloss = cast volume x loss rate = 100,000 hL x 0.08 = 8,000 hL

At 400 hL per brew, that is 8,000 / 400 = 20 brews a year that never reach a customer.

Splitting that 8,000 hL by stage is the first project. If the cellar accounts for 2 percentage points, that is 2,000 hL, or five brews, lost between fermenter and bright tank. That tells the head brewer whether to look at yeast handling, tank bottoms, transfers or filtration. It also tells finance what one point of loss is worth, which is the sum every later project gets judged against.

The trap is the denominator. Some breweries measure loss against cast wort, some against beer in fermenter, some against brewed volume corrected for temperature. Pick one, write it down and have finance sign it. That signature is the baseline.

Who owns what and what good looks like

A brewery data strategy is not an IT project. The head brewer owns the brewing definitions. The packaging lead owns line data and stoppage codes. Quality owns the lab and the specification. Finance owns cost and excise. IT owns access. Someone has to own the batch record across all of them. In a mid-sized brewery that is often the person who already keeps the loss spreadsheet. Give them the time and the title.

Be wary of buying the big piece first. A new MES, a historian upgrade or a platform licence may all be right eventually, but none of them tells you which batch lost the beer. Prove the batch record and one question on the systems you already have. The gaps you hit, such as a filler that does not log stoppage causes, become a precise shopping list instead of a vendor's wish list.

Before you sign with anyone, ask four things. Which number will move and who signs the baseline? What runs in our tenant and what stays with you? What do we own if we stop? Does anything write back to our control systems? If the answers are vague, so is the strategy.

With one question that costs money every week, such as beer loss by stage or fermentation time, plus the batch record needed to answer it. Platforms, dashboards and AI come after that record is trusted.
